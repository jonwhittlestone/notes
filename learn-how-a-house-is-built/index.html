What this is

BuildRow is an isometric construction plot where every station is one stage of building a single 5-bedroom brick house in the UK. A truck leaves the site office with nothing but a set of plans: it waits out the planning clock, cuts and pours the ground, builds the blockwork inner leaf and roof frame, fits the openings, and runs electrics, plumbing and heating first fix before a building control inspector checks the rough-in. A crew that was rushed and cheap gets sent down a rework loop; everyone else goes straight to insulation, plasterboard, the finishes, the second fix and the garden.

The gauge on the truck's flank is the fraction of the whole budget it has spent so far. The crates in its bed are that station's own material spend, one crate per £4,000. Both are read straight out of the model.

How much of it is real

Genuinely computed, live, in your browser: every quantity on the panel — cubic metres of concrete, blockwork and brickwork area, structural timber, roof tile area, wire and pipe length, plasterboard sheets — is derived from the house's own floor area and geometry, not looked up. Cost, labour-hours and calendar days are built from those quantities and the Crew size, Finish level and Region sliders. The rough-in inspection is a real branch: a crew smaller than four finishing a basic-spec job fails, every other combination passes, decided fresh every run. It is all in js/model.js, about 300 lines, runnable on its own in node.

Scaled down: nothing here is scaled down the way a token count or a clock speed would be — a house is already a size a reader can look at directly.

Assumed: every unit cost, labour-productivity rate and rule-of-thumb ratio in the model — structural timber per square metre, bathrooms per bedroom count, wire length per square metre, and every pound figure. Builders do not publish takeoffs, so these are 2025 UK national ballpark figures, each marked // ASSUMED at its point of definition in model.js. The model also prices materials and trade labour only — it leaves out land, utility hookups and the main contractor's own margin, which is most of why an all-in quote runs well above the £/sqm figure on this panel.

Indicative: the schedule assumes every trade works back to back with no gap — several (electrics, plumbing, heating first fix) really do run in parallel on a real job, and real schedules lose additional weeks to weather and material lead times that this model does not charge for. Treat the calendar-days total as an optimistic floor, not a promise. The building shapes, the part counts and every dimension of the machinery on the plot are illustration, not a real takeoff drawing — treat the plot as scenery and the numbers on the panel as the lesson.

Pacing

The first time the truck reaches a station it stops for long enough to read that station's write-up — between 9 and 26 seconds, depending on how much there is to say — and a progress bar under the panel text shows how much of the stop is left. Once every station has been explained there is nothing new to read, so a later run moves at a watchable pace instead of a readable one. Space holds any stop indefinitely, S steps one station at a time, and the Speed slider scales everything, reading stops included. Reset (⟲) replays the slow tour; Run keeps what you have already read.

Controls

  • Space play / pause · S advance one station · R reset and replay the tour · F follow camera · L labels
  • Drag to pan, scroll to zoom, click any station for its write-up.
  • The view starts close on the truck and rides with it. The ⤢ button on the left, or a double-click on the map, pulls back to the whole plot.
