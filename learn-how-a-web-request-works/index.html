What this is

One keypress, followed all the way to the light coming off your screen. A trolley leaves a key switch carrying a closed circuit, climbs to the application plateau as the signal becomes a byte and then a URL, descends six terraces as every OSI layer wraps it in another header, crosses a very long wire, climbs the same six terraces in reverse on the far side, is answered by a datacentre, and comes home on a viaduct to a monitor that will not accept it until its next refresh.

The geography is the argument. Going up means rising in abstraction; going down means handing your message to something that understands less of it than you do. The shells stacked on the trolley are the actual encapsulation state — one ring per header currently applied — and its readout is the actual byte count at that point in the stack.

How much of it is real

Genuinely computed, live, in your browser — all of it in js/model.js, about 400 lines. The whole byte ledger: a real if abbreviated HPACK encoder over nine headers a browser actually sends, with static-table matches costing one byte, name-indexed literals costing an index plus a length plus a Huffman-coded value, and a dynamic table that collapses everything but the path on a reused connection. Then the exact standard-mandated headers on top of it: 9 bytes of HTTP/2 frame, 22 for a TLS 1.3 record (5 header, 1 inner content type, 16 AEAD tag), 32 for TCP with timestamp options, 20 or 40 for IPv4 or IPv6, 18 for Ethernet, padding to the 60-byte minimum frame, and 20 more of preamble and interframe gap. The maximum segment size falls out of the MTU minus those headers.

And the whole time waterfall: mean wait for the next keyboard matrix sweep and the next USB poll, both derived from the rates you set; the 8-byte HID report at 12 Mbps; propagation at the real speed of light in silica (299,792.458 km/s divided by a group index of 1.4675, so 204,287 km/s); serialisation of the actual byte count onto your actual uplink; setup round trips charged at one for TCP and one or two for TLS depending on the version; index fan-out tail probabilities as an exact 1 − (1 − p)shards over two tail tiers; brotli compression; TCP slow start from a ten-segment initial window, doubling each round, taking whichever of bandwidth and round trips binds; and the mean wait plus scan-out time for your display refresh rate. Every displayed number is one of those calculations.

Scaled down: twenty index halls stand in for however many shards the slider says (real search runs thousands); the plate stacks show one plate per four bytes; the trolley's shells are six, matching the six terraces, where a real packet's headers are not visibly nested at all.

Assumed — real arithmetic sitting on a number nobody publishes, each one marked // ASSUMED where it is defined. Fibre follows a path 1.4× the map distance. A router costs 0.12 ms. HPACK Huffman gets ASCII to 78%. Debounce is 5 ms. Kernel and compositor dispatch is 0.9 ms. A shard's median is 6 ms, with a 1% chance of a 55 ms tail and a 0.04% chance of a 240 ms stall. A shard holds 8 million documents and returns 40 candidates. Brotli gets search HTML down 4.2×. Your uplink is a fifth of your downlink. Parse, style, paint and pixel-response costs per kilobyte. The cookie and user-agent strings are realistic in shape and length but are not anybody's real values.

Deliberately simplified, and you would otherwise assume otherwise: a real page load is dozens of requests over several connections, not one. The response's own trip down the server's stack and back up yours is not walked — the trolley is simply re-wrapped at the Ranking Floor and unwrapped at the Screen Works — so the byte ledger you see is the request's, not the response's. DNS resolution is charged as one round trip rather than driven as its own journey, though it is exactly the same journey. The results cache is placed at the frontend; the real thing is more layered than that. And the datacentre is one building here: in reality your query crosses several more networks inside it than it did to get there.

Scenery: the grass, the trees, the background blocks and the km posts. They are there so the mechanism has somewhere to happen. Treat the panel as the lesson and the landscape as an illustration — but note that the landmarks are not scenery: the plate stacks, the hopper mouth, the lit shard halls, the congestion-window gantries and the trolley's shells are all drawn from the model and change when you change it.

Pacing

The first time the trolley reaches a station it stops for as long as that station's write-up takes to read — between 9 and 26 seconds — and the bar under the panel text shows how much of the stop is left. The full first tour is about seven and a half minutes, which is what eighteen stations spanning a switch contact to a photon costs; it is longer than these things usually run, and the Speed slider scales all of it, reading stops included. Space holds any stop indefinitely and S steps one station at a time.

Once every district has been explained there is nothing new to read, so the remaining runs go at a watchable pace instead of a readable one. Reset (⟲) replays the slow tour; Run keeps what you have already read.

Three keystrokes run in a row on purpose. The second and third are much cheaper and nothing about the network changed: DNS is answered, the connection is open so there are no handshake round trips, and HPACK's dynamic table has every header but the path. Watch the request shrink on the byte ledger.

Things worth trying

  • Put Distance at 16,000 km, then drag Connection from 1 to 1000 Mbps. The transfer bar barely moves: it is round trips, not bandwidth.
  • Toggle IPv6 and watch the L3 plate stack grow and the usable segment size shrink by exactly the same 20 bytes.
  • Drag Index shards from 1 to 4000. Nothing about the network changed, and a bar grew by 200 ms. That is the tail-at-scale problem.
  • Type a much longer query. Only the path grows — everything else is already indexed.
  • Put USB poll at 1000 Hz and Refresh at 240 Hz to see how much of the total was never network at all.
